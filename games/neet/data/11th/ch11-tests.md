## Concept Index — Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

**Paper 1 (Q1–Q30): What Do We Know / Early Experiments — historical basis of photosynthesis**

1. Variegated/partially covered leaf experiment shows photosynthesis occurs only in green parts in the presence of light.
2. KOH-soaked cotton experiment shows CO₂ is required for photosynthesis.
3. Joseph Priestley (1733–1804) performed bell jar experiments in 1770; discovered oxygen in 1774.
4. A burning candle or a suffocating mouse in a sealed bell jar is revived when a mint plant is placed in it.
5. Priestley's hypothesis: plants restore to the air whatever breathing animals and burning candles remove.
6. Jan Ingenhousz (1730–1799) showed that sunlight is essential for the plant's air-purifying process.
7. Ingenhousz's aquatic plant experiment: bubbles of oxygen form around green parts only in bright sunlight, not in the dark.
8. Ingenhousz identified the bubbles as oxygen and showed only green parts release it.
9. Julius von Sachs (~1854) provided evidence for glucose production during plant growth, usually stored as starch.
10. Sachs showed the green substance (chlorophyll) is located in special bodies, later called chloroplasts.
11. T.W. Engelmann (1843–1909) used a prism to split light and illuminated the alga Cladophora with aerobic bacteria.
12. Bacteria were used to detect the sites of O₂ evolution; they accumulated in blue and red light regions.
13. Engelmann's experiment produced the first action spectrum of photosynthesis, resembling chlorophyll a and b absorption spectra.
14. By the mid-nineteenth century, the empirical equation was CO₂ + H₂O --light--> [CH₂O] + O₂.
15. Cornelius van Niel (1897–1985) studied purple and green bacteria to understand photosynthesis as a light-dependent redox reaction.
16. Van Niel's general equation: 2H₂A + CO₂ --light--> [CH₂O] + H₂O + 2A.
17. In green plants, H₂O is the hydrogen donor and is oxidised to O₂.
18. In purple/green sulphur bacteria using H₂S as donor, the oxidation product is sulphur or sulphate, not O₂.
19. Van Niel inferred that O₂ evolved by green plants comes from H₂O, not CO₂; later proved by radioisotopic techniques.
20. The correct overall equation: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O --light--> C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O.
21. Photosynthesis is a multistep process, not a single reaction.
22. Twelve molecules of water are used as substrate, though six are effectively regenerated.

**Paper 2 (Q1–Q30): Where Photosynthesis Takes Place / Pigments Involved**

1. Photosynthesis occurs in green leaves and other green parts of the plant.
2. Mesophyll cells have a large number of chloroplasts, aligned along cell walls for optimum light.
3. Chloroplast has a membranous system: grana, stroma lamellae and matrix stroma.
4. The membrane system traps light energy and synthesises ATP and NADPH.
5. In the stroma, enzymatic reactions synthesise sugar, which forms starch.
6. Light-driven reactions are called light reactions (photochemical reactions).
7. Reactions dependent on light-reaction products are called dark reactions (carbon reactions) by convention.
8. "Dark reaction" does not mean the reactions occur in darkness or are not light-dependent.
9. Paper chromatography of leaf pigments reveals four pigments.
10. Chlorophyll a appears bright/blue-green; chlorophyll b appears yellow-green.
11. Xanthophylls appear yellow; carotenoids appear yellow to yellow-orange.
12. Pigments absorb light at specific wavelengths.
13. Chlorophyll a is the most abundant plant pigment and the chief pigment associated with photosynthesis.
14. Chlorophyll a shows maximum absorption in blue and red regions, matching higher photosynthesis rates.
15. There is no complete one-to-one overlap between chlorophyll a's absorption spectrum and the action spectrum.
16. Most photosynthesis occurs in blue and red regions of the spectrum; some occurs at other wavelengths.
17. Chlorophyll b, xanthophylls and carotenoids are accessory pigments.
18. Accessory pigments absorb light and transfer the energy to chlorophyll a.
19. Accessory pigments widen the usable range of wavelengths for photosynthesis.
20. Accessory pigments protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.
21. The colour seen in leaves is due to four pigments, not a single pigment.

**Paper 3 (Q1–Q30): Light Reaction / Electron Transport / Chemiosmotic Hypothesis**

1. Light reactions include light absorption, water splitting, oxygen release, and formation of ATP and NADPH.
2. Pigments are organised into two light harvesting complexes (LHC) within PS I and PS II.
3. PS I and PS II are named in order of discovery, not order of function.
4. Each photosystem has an antenna of pigments and one reaction centre chlorophyll a molecule.
5. PS I reaction centre chlorophyll a (P700) has absorption peak at 700 nm.
6. PS II reaction centre chlorophyll a (P680) has absorption maxima at 680 nm.
7. PS II absorbs 680 nm light, exciting electrons that pass to an electron acceptor and then to cytochromes.
8. Electron movement from PS II to PS I is downhill on the redox potential scale.
9. Electrons in PS I are excited by 700 nm light and transferred to an acceptor of greater redox potential, then downhill to NADP⁺.
10. This overall transfer of electrons is called the Z scheme.
11. Splitting of water is associated with PS II; water splits into 2H⁺, [O] and electrons.
12. The water splitting complex is located on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane.
13. Electrons from water splitting replace those lost from PS II.
14. Photo-phosphorylation is ATP synthesis from ADP and inorganic phosphate in the presence of light.
15. Non-cyclic photophosphorylation: PS II and PS I work in series, producing both ATP and NADPH + H⁺.
16. Cyclic photophosphorylation: only PS I functional, electron circulated, producing only ATP.
17. Cyclic photophosphorylation likely occurs in the stroma lamellae, which lack PS II and NADP reductase.
18. Cyclic photophosphorylation also occurs when only light beyond 680 nm is available.
19. The chemiosmotic hypothesis explains ATP synthesis via a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane.
20. Protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen (inside), unlike in mitochondria where they accumulate in the intermembrane space.
21. Water splitting, electron transport (via an H-carrier primary acceptor), and NADP⁺ reduction all contribute to the proton gradient.
22. Breakdown of the gradient through CF₀ causes a conformational change in CF₁, synthesising ATP.
23. Chemiosmosis requires a membrane, a proton pump, a proton gradient and ATP synthase.

**Paper 4 (Q1–Q30): Biosynthetic Phase (ATP/NADPH Use) and the Calvin Cycle**

1. Products of the light reaction are ATP, NADPH and O₂; O₂ diffuses out, ATP and NADPH drive the biosynthetic phase.
2. The biosynthetic phase depends on ATP, NADPH, CO₂ and H₂O, not directly on light.
3. The biosynthetic process continues briefly after light is removed, then stops, and resumes when light returns.
4. Calling the biosynthetic phase a "dark reaction" may be a misnomer.
5. Melvin Calvin used radioactive ¹⁴C in algal photosynthesis studies.
6. The first CO₂ fixation product discovered was the 3-carbon acid 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA).
7. Another group of plants showed a 4-carbon first product, oxaloacetic acid (OAA).
8. C₃ pathway: first product is PGA; C₄ pathway: first product is OAA.
9. The primary CO₂ acceptor in the Calvin cycle is the 5-carbon ketose sugar RuBP.
10. Scientists initially expected a 2-carbon acceptor before discovering RuBP.
11. The Calvin pathway operates cyclically, regenerating RuBP, and occurs in all photosynthetic plants.
12. The Calvin cycle has three stages: carboxylation, reduction and regeneration.
13. Carboxylation: CO₂ combines with RuBP, catalysed by RuBP carboxylase, forming two molecules of 3-PGA.
14. The enzyme is more correctly called RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase (RuBisCO) due to its oxygenation activity.
15. Reduction: uses 2 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂ fixed to form sugars.
16. Six CO₂ molecules and six turns of the cycle are required to form one glucose molecule.
17. Regeneration: reforms RuBP using one ATP; essential for the cycle to continue.
18. Every CO₂ molecule fixed requires 3 ATP and 2 NADPH overall.
19. Cyclic photophosphorylation may help meet the extra ATP requirement relative to NADPH.
20. In/Out summary: 6 CO₂ + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH → 1 glucose + 18 ADP + 12 NADP.

**Paper 5 (Q1–Q30): The C₄ Pathway, Photorespiration and Factors Affecting Photosynthesis**

1. C₄ plants are adapted to dry tropical regions.
2. C₄ plants show special leaf anatomy, higher temperature tolerance, response to high light, no photorespiration, and greater biomass productivity.
3. Kranz anatomy ('Kranz' = wreath) features large bundle sheath cells around vascular bundles.
4. Bundle sheath cells: many chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gas exchange, no intercellular spaces.
5. The C₄ pathway is also called the Hatch and Slack Pathway, a cyclic process.
6. Primary CO₂ acceptor in C₄ plants is PEP (3-carbon), fixed by PEP carboxylase (PEPcase) in mesophyll cells.
7. Mesophyll cells lack RuBisCO; OAA forms in mesophyll cells and is converted to malic/aspartic acid.
8. These 4-carbon acids are transported to bundle sheath cells and broken down, releasing CO₂ and a 3-carbon molecule.
9. The 3-carbon molecule returns to mesophyll cells to regenerate PEP.
10. CO₂ released in bundle sheath cells enters the Calvin pathway; bundle sheath cells are rich in RuBisCO but lack PEPcase.
11. In C₄ plants, the Calvin pathway occurs only in bundle sheath cells, not mesophyll cells.
12. Photorespiration: RuBisCO's active site binds both CO₂ and O₂ competitively.
13. In C₃ plants, RuBP binding O₂ forms phosphoglycerate and phosphoglycolate, releasing CO₂ with ATP use but no sugar/ATP/NADPH synthesis.
14. The biological function of photorespiration is not known.
15. C₄ plants avoid photorespiration by raising CO₂ concentration at the RuBisCO site in bundle sheath cells.
16. Internal factors affecting photosynthesis: leaf/mesophyll/chloroplast number, size, age, orientation, internal CO₂, chlorophyll amount.
17. External factors: sunlight, temperature, CO₂ concentration, water.
18. Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905): rate is set by the factor nearest its minimal value.
19. Light saturation occurs at about 10% of full sunlight; light is rarely limiting except in shade/dense forest.
20. Excess incident light can cause chlorophyll breakdown and decreased photosynthesis.
21. CO₂ is the major limiting factor; atmospheric concentration is only 0.03–0.04%.
22. C₄ plants saturate at ~360 µl/L CO₂; C₃ plants saturate only beyond 450 µl/L, making current CO₂ levels limiting for C₃ plants.
23. Greenhouse crops (tomato, bell pepper) are grown in CO₂-enriched atmospheres for higher yield.
24. Dark reactions are temperature-controlled (enzymatic); light reactions are less temperature-sensitive.
25. C₄ plants respond to higher temperatures; C₃ plants have a lower temperature optimum.
26. Temperature optimum depends on habitat; tropical plants have a higher optimum than temperate plants.
27. Water stress reduces photosynthesis mainly indirectly: closing stomata (less CO₂) and wilting leaves (less surface area/metabolic activity).
