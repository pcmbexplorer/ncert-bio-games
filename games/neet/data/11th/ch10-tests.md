## Concept Index — Cell Cycle and Cell Division

**Paper 1 (Q1–Q30): The Cell Cycle — Phases and Regulation**
1. Cell cycle — sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises constituents, and divides
2. Two basic phases — Interphase and M Phase
3. Human cell cycle duration — approximately 24 hours
4. Yeast cell cycle duration — approximately 90 minutes
5. Cell division proper lasts only about an hour of the 24-hour human cycle
6. Interphase lasts more than 95% of the cell cycle duration
7. M Phase starts with karyokinesis, usually ends with cytokinesis
8. Interphase as the "resting phase" — preparation through cell growth and DNA replication
9. Interphase sub-phases — G₁, S, G₂
10. G₁ phase — interval between mitosis and DNA replication initiation, cell grows
11. S phase — DNA synthesis/replication, DNA content doubles (2C→4C)
12. Chromosome number unchanged after S phase (2n remains 2n)
13. Centriole duplication in cytoplasm during S phase (animal cells)
14. G₂ phase — protein synthesis for mitosis, continued cell growth
15. Non-dividing adult cells (e.g., heart cells) and occasional dividers
16. G₀ (quiescent) stage — metabolically active, not proliferating
17. Mitotic division restricted to diploid somatic cells in animals (with exceptions, e.g., male honey bees)
18. Plants show mitotic division in both haploid and diploid cells

**Paper 2 (Q1–Q30): M Phase — Prophase and Metaphase**
1. Mitosis called equational division (chromosome number conserved)
2. Mitosis divided into four stages for convenience; a progressive process
3. Karyokinesis stages — Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase
4. Prophase follows S and G₂ phases
5. New DNA molecules intertwined (not distinct) in S/G₂
6. Chromatin condensation during prophase
7. Chromosomes composed of two chromatids joined at centromere
8. Centrosome duplication (S phase) and movement to opposite poles
9. Asters — microtubules radiating from centrosome
10. Mitotic apparatus — two asters plus spindle fibres
11. Onion root tip — 16 chromosomes (example)
12. Absence of golgi, ER, nucleolus, nuclear envelope at end of prophase
13. Metaphase begins with complete nuclear envelope disintegration
14. Metaphase — stage for easiest study of chromosome morphology
15. Metaphase chromosome — two sister chromatids held by centromere
16. Kinetochores — disc-shaped structures, spindle fibre attachment sites
17. Metaphase plate — plane of chromosome alignment at the equator
18. Opposite-pole attachment of sister chromatid kinetochores

**Paper 3 (Q1–Q30): Anaphase, Telophase, Cytokinesis and Significance of Mitosis**
1. Anaphase — simultaneous splitting of each chromosome at the metaphase plate
2. Daughter chromatids termed daughter chromosomes after splitting
3. Centromere leads, arms trail during poleward chromatid movement
4. Telophase — chromosomes decondense, lose individuality at poles
5. Nuclear envelope reforms around chromosome clusters, forming two daughter nuclei
6. Nucleolus, golgi complex and ER reform at telophase
7. Cytokinesis — division of cytoplasm into two daughter cells
8. Animal cell cytokinesis — furrow in plasma membrane deepens and joins centrally
9. Plant cell cytokinesis — cell wall formation from centre outward
10. Cell plate — precursor structure, represents middle lamella
11. Distribution of mitochondria and plastids between daughter cells
12. Syncytium — multinucleate condition from karyokinesis without cytokinesis
13. Liquid endosperm of coconut — example of syncytium
14. Mitosis usually restricted to diploid cells (exceptions in lower plants/social insects)
15. Mitosis produces diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement
16. Growth of multicellular organisms due to mitosis
17. Nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio restoration through cell division
18. Cell repair — epidermis, gut lining, blood cells constantly replaced
19. Meristematic tissue (apical and lateral cambium) — continuous plant growth

**Paper 4 (Q1–Q30): Meiosis I**
1. Sexual reproduction — fusion of two haploid gametes
2. Gametes formed from specialised diploid cells
3. Meiosis — reduces chromosome number by half, produces haploid cells
4. Meiosis ensures haploid phase; fertilisation restores diploid phase
5. Two sequential divisions (meiosis I, II), single DNA replication cycle
6. Meiosis I initiated after chromosome replication at S phase
7. Pairing of homologous chromosomes and recombination between non-sister chromatids
8. Four haploid cells formed at end of meiosis II
9. Prophase I — five sub-stages: leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, diakinesis
10. Leptotene — chromosomes gradually become visible, compaction continues
11. Zygotene — synapsis, synaptonemal complex formation
12. Bivalent/tetrad — complex of paired homologous chromosomes
13. Pachytene — four chromatids distinct as tetrads
14. Recombination nodules — sites of crossing over
15. Crossing over — exchange of genetic material, enzyme recombinase
16. Diplotene — synaptonemal complex dissolves, chromosomes separate except at chiasmata
17. Chiasmata — X-shaped structures at crossover sites
18. Diplotene duration in oocytes of some vertebrates (months/years)
19. Diakinesis — terminalisation of chiasmata, nucleolus disappears, nuclear envelope breaks down
20. Metaphase I — bivalents align on equatorial plate
21. Anaphase I — homologous chromosomes separate, sister chromatids stay joined
22. Telophase I — nuclear membrane/nucleolus reappear, dyad of cells forms
23. Interkinesis — short-lived stage between meiotic divisions, no DNA replication

**Paper 5 (Q1–Q30): Meiosis II and Significance of Meiosis**
1. Prophase II initiated immediately after cytokinesis of meiosis I
2. Meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis
3. Nuclear membrane disappears by end of prophase II
4. Chromosomes become compact again in prophase II
5. Metaphase II — chromosomes align at equator, sister chromatid kinetochores attach
6. Anaphase II — simultaneous splitting of centromeres
7. Chromatid movement via shortening of kinetochore-attached microtubules
8. Telophase II — nuclear envelope reforms, cytokinesis follows
9. Tetrad of cells — four haploid daughter cells at end of meiosis
10. Meiosis conserves species-specific chromosome number across generations
11. Paradox — chromosome number halved yet conserved across generations via fertilisation
12. Meiosis increases genetic variability in a population
13. Genetic variability important for the process of evolution
